# Keep a friction log

Most systems do not fail with a cinematic error message. They fail by asking for one unnecessary click at a time.

A form forgets your previous answer. A meeting begins without the document everyone needs. A chapter opens at the cover instead of the place you left. None of these moments is important enough to trigger a redesign by itself. Together they become the texture of a tool.

## Small delays are product decisions

When people say a product feels “fast,” they are often describing more than network speed. They mean the product remembers context. It keeps the next action nearby. It does not make the user renegotiate decisions already made.

A friction log is a deliberately low-tech way to notice this.

For one week, write down every moment when you think one of these sentences:

- I already told it that.
- Why am I here again?
- Where did that setting go?
- I know this is possible, but I cannot see how.
- I am doing the machine's bookkeeping for it.

Do not fix the problems while you are logging them. Observation and repair use different kinds of attention.

> Repeated minor inconvenience is not minor. Repetition is the multiplier.

## Classify before you optimize

At the end of the week, sort the notes into three buckets.

**Memory friction** happens when the system forgets context the person reasonably expects it to retain.

**Navigation friction** happens when the next meaningful action is hidden, displaced, or expressed in the system's language instead of the user's.

**Decision friction** happens when the interface presents choices that should have been defaults, constraints, or consequences.

This classification matters because the tempting fix is often wrong. A navigation problem does not need a faster server. A decision problem does not need another tooltip. A memory problem does not need a tutorial telling people to remember for the software.

## Remove one thing

The first pass should be subtraction.

Can a step disappear? Can a sensible default replace a question? Can a preference remain local to the person who chose it? Can the interface show the current source of a setting instead of making the user guess?

Good systems feel clear partly because they are willing to do quiet work on the reader's behalf.

The margin is useful not because it is empty, but because emptiness gives the important thing somewhere to be seen.
