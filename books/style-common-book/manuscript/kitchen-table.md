# The kitchen table

The table had belonged to three apartments before it belonged to Mara. One leg was shorter than the others, the varnish had gone pale where hot plates had landed without permission, and somebody—possibly her brother, possibly a previous tenant—had cut a small crescent into the edge with a bread knife.

None of this made it valuable. That was the point.

On Sunday morning Mara set two mugs on it and unfolded the map her father had mailed from the coast. He still distrusted links sent by text. If a road mattered, he believed, it ought to arrive creased.

“Does the ferry even run in October?” Eli asked.

“It runs when it feels like it.”

“That is not a schedule.”

“It is an island schedule.”

He leaned over the map. The paper made a dry, private sound beneath his sleeve. Outside, a delivery truck reversed somewhere down the block, three warning notes repeating until they became part of the morning.

Mara traced the route with one finger: highway, smaller highway, two-lane road, ferry, then the last six miles along the north shore. On the map the trip looked simple enough to fit under her hand.

In life it had taken eleven years.

She had left the island at nineteen with two suitcases and an argument she had rehearsed so often that, by the time she used it, she no longer knew which parts she believed. She had returned for weddings and a funeral, always sleeping in the room with the blue curtains, always leaving before the silence could become a conversation.

This time was different only because her father had written one sentence below the map.

> Come while the pears are still on the tree.

Eli read it twice.

“Is that how he says he misses you?”

Mara turned her mug in a slow circle. “That is practically a speech.”

They sat without deciding anything. The table held the map open between them. Morning light moved across the grain and caught in the scratches.

A good page can work like that table. It does not ask to be admired before it can be used. It gives the eye enough room to stay, enough rhythm to continue, enough structure that the reader stops noticing the structure at all.

Mara folded the map along its old lines.

“October?” Eli asked.

She nodded.

“October.”
