# A page you can change

A reading interface should not ask you to prove that you need a change.

If larger text helps, use larger text.

If a dark page is easier at night, use a dark page.

If justified text makes it harder to keep your place, choose left alignment.

These are reading preferences, not mistakes.

## Start with comfort

There is no single best setting for every person, screen, room, or book.

A useful Reader gives you several independent choices:

- **Text size** for the scale of the letters.
- **Typeface** for the shapes of the letters.
- **Line spacing** for vertical breathing room.
- **Measure** for how long each line becomes.
- **Alignment** for the shape of the right edge.
- **Color and contrast** for the page and ink.
- **Pages or Scroll** for how you move through the text.

Change one thing at a time. Read a few paragraphs. Keep the setting if it helps.

## Your choice should stay yours

An author can recommend a starting design. That recommendation can be useful. A mystery may open in a dark atmosphere. A textbook may begin with a narrow measure. A novel may choose a serif face and traditional indentation.

But a recommendation should not become a lock.

When you change the Reader, your browser should remember **your** choice. The book file should not change. Another reader should not inherit your settings. The author should not receive a hidden signal telling them what you chose.

This separation is simple and important:

> The publication can suggest. The reader decides.

## A practical check

If reading feels tiring, try this order:

1. Increase the text size.
2. Increase the line spacing.
3. Narrow the line length.
4. Switch to left alignment.
5. Try a clearer typeface.
6. Increase contrast or choose a calmer page color.

You do not need to keep the author's design to read the author's words correctly.

The content belongs to the publication.

The reading experience belongs to you.
