# A letter folded twice

By late afternoon the rain had thinned into a silver mist, and the windows of the west room held the garden as if it were something remembered rather than seen. Iris lit the green-shaded lamp on the desk though there was still daylight enough to read by. The lamp made a smaller weather, a circle of amber in which paper, brass, and the veins in her hands seemed suddenly more exact.

The letter had waited beneath the clock for six days.

She knew the handwriting before she had opened the envelope. Daniel still crossed his sevens as though numbers might otherwise escape him, and his capital *I* leaned forward with the impatience he had never learned to disguise in person.

She unfolded the page once, then again.

> I keep thinking about the station clock. I was certain it had stopped. You said it was only slow.

That was all he wrote about the morning they had last seen each other.

Outside, water slipped from the yew branches in heavy drops. Somewhere beyond the wall a bicycle bell sounded twice and was gone. Iris read the sentence again, not because it was difficult but because simple sentences sometimes carry the most furniture.

The clock had been slow. Seven minutes, exactly. Daniel had insisted the whole station was conspiring to make him miss the northbound train, and she had laughed because at twenty-seven she believed there would always be another train, another platform, another afternoon on which the important thing might be said properly.

There are mistakes that announce themselves with broken glass. Others are made of ordinary minutes.

Iris turned the page.

Daniel wrote about the sea wall, the apricot tree he had planted badly and then rescued, the neighbor's dog that had adopted his kitchen step. He wrote that his daughter had started university. He wrote, almost at the end, that he had been ill and was now less ill. The sentence was plain and therefore frightening.

Then came the final paragraph.

> If you ever come west, the blue room still catches the evening light. I have not changed the curtains. I suppose this is my way of admitting that some kinds of waiting are just another form of hope.

The lamp hummed softly.

Iris laid the letter flat and placed both palms beside it, as though warming them at a very small fire. The room had darkened around the circle of light without her noticing. In the window, her own reflection had appeared over the garden.

She reached for a fresh sheet of paper.

For several minutes she did not write. She listened instead to the rain finishing itself on the roof, to the clock on the mantel gaining and losing the same second, to the almost unbearable fact that the evening had not yet ended.

Then she began with his name.
