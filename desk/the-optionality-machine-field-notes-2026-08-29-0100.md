# THE OPTIONALITY MACHINE — FIELD NOTES

## Twenty Practices for Preserving Better Futures

### Sven Hard Benson

The main manuscript explains the theory. The casebook shows the mechanisms. These field notes turn the ideas into repeatable practice.

The objective is not to maximize choices. The objective is to preserve enough high-quality choices that commitment can become more intelligent.

---

## Practice 1 — Classify the Decision Before Solving It

Before debating what to do, classify the decision.

Ask:

- Is it reversible or difficult to reverse?
- Is feedback fast or slow?
- Is downside capped or existential?
- Will meaningful new information arrive if we wait?
- Does delay itself destroy value?

Many meetings are inefficient because people argue about conclusions before agreeing on the type of decision.

A reversible decision with fast feedback should usually move toward action.

An irreversible decision with slow feedback deserves more modeling, disagreement, and evidence.

Write the classification at the top of the decision document.

The classification changes the burden of proof.

---

## Practice 2 — Buy Information, Not Comfort

When uncertainty feels uncomfortable, organizations often purchase analysis that sounds sophisticated but does not change any action.

This is comfort, not information.

Before commissioning research, ask:

**What decision would change depending on the result?**

If no plausible result changes the decision, do not pretend the analysis is decision support.

If the decision can change, specify thresholds in advance.

For example:

- If customer willingness to pay exceeds X, continue.
- If defect rate remains above Y, redesign.
- If acquisition cost does not fall below Z, stop scaling.

Information has value when it changes capital allocation, behavior, or commitment.

---

## Practice 3 — Shrink the Bet Until You Can Learn

A large decision can often be decomposed into a smaller experiment.

Instead of launching nationally, test one city.

Instead of changing the entire technology stack, replace one service.

Instead of quitting immediately, take one client.

Instead of writing a book, publish one essay.

Instead of purchasing a factory, contract a small run.

The key question is:

**What is the smallest action that can produce evidence relevant to the larger decision?**

Beware experiments so small they test nothing important.

A good pilot preserves the causal structure of the real problem while reducing cost and irreversibility.

---

## Practice 4 — Define the Kill Criteria While You Still Love the Idea

Every project becomes harder to stop after people, prestige, and money accumulate.

Define exit criteria before sunk cost takes control.

Write down:

- what must become true,
- by when,
- what evidence would falsify the thesis,
- what budget is the maximum learning cost,
- who has authority to stop the project.

A kill criterion is not a promise to behave mechanically. Reality can justify exceptions.

Its purpose is to prevent the standard of success from quietly moving every time the project disappoints.

If the team cannot imagine any evidence that would cause it to stop, the project is no longer an experiment.

It is a belief system.

## Practice 5 — Map Single Points of Failure

Draw the system.

Circle anything whose failure stops the whole process.

Supplier.

Employee.

Credential.

Technology vendor.

Distribution channel.

Bank.

Country.

Customer.

Database.

Power source.

Relationship.

Then ask three questions:

1. How likely is failure?
2. How severe is failure?
3. How long would substitution take?

The third question is often ignored.

A dependency can be acceptable when substitution takes one day and dangerous when substitution takes two years.

Optionality is partly the speed with which alternatives can become real.

---

## Practice 6 — Maintain the Backup by Using It

A backup that never runs may not be a backup.

Send a small amount of volume through the secondary supplier.

Restore from backups periodically.

Let the deputy lead meetings.

Run the generator.

Practice the emergency procedure.

Use the second payment processor.

Call the old customer channel.

Speak the language you claim to know.

Exercise turns theoretical optionality into operational optionality.

This costs money and time.

That is the maintenance premium of resilience.

---

## Practice 7 — Separate Mission, Strategy, and Tactics

Write three lines.

**Mission:** What enduring outcome matters?

**Strategy:** What current theory explains how to achieve it?

**Tactics:** What actions implement the theory now?

Then label disagreements correctly.

Changing tactics should be easy.

Changing strategy should require evidence.

Changing mission should be rare and explicit.

Many organizations become rigid because a tactic acquires moral status.

“We have always sold through partners” becomes identity.

“We are a hardware company” becomes destiny.

“We never discount” becomes doctrine.

Hierarchical commitment preserves values while keeping methods adaptive.

---

## Practice 8 — Keep a Decision Journal

Before a consequential choice, record:

- what you believe,
- how confident you are,
- what evidence you used,
- what alternatives you considered,
- what outcome you expect,
- what would surprise you,
- what would cause you to revise.

Return later.

Do not grade the decision only by outcome.

A good process can produce a bad outcome under uncertainty.

A reckless process can get lucky.

The journal protects learning from hindsight bias.

It also reveals recurring errors in your own forecasting.

The objective is not to prove you were smart.

It is to become better calibrated.

---

## Practice 9 — Run a Pre-Mortem

Assume the project failed spectacularly one year from now.

Ask everyone independently:

**What happened?**

The fictional failure changes social incentives.

Instead of sounding negative, participants are completing the exercise.

Hidden concerns surface.

The best pre-mortems identify preventable failure modes before commitment hardens.

Do not turn the exercise into pessimistic theater.

Rank risks by severity, likelihood, detectability, and ability to mitigate.

Then create specific monitoring signals.

A risk nobody watches is merely a paragraph.

---

## Practice 10 — Run a Pre-Parade

The opposite exercise is also useful.

Assume the project succeeds beyond expectations.

What breaks?

Capacity?

Hiring?

Cash flow?

Customer support?

Power?

Supply?

Reputation?

Regulatory attention?

Success creates bottlenecks.

Organizations often prepare for failure and are surprised by growth.

An option on success must be maintained too.

Can the system scale without destroying what made it valuable?

Optionality is not only about escape.

It is also about absorption.

---

## Practice 11 — Protect One Weird Bet

Reserve a small amount of time or capital for an idea that does not fit the current model.

Not a random idea.

A coherent idea whose uncertainty is high and upside meaningful.

The weird bet should have:

- limited downside,
- an owner,
- a learning objective,
- a review date,
- permission to fail.

This protects variation inside successful organizations.

Most weird bets should die.

If none die, the portfolio is too safe.

If all are enormous, the portfolio is reckless.

The point is to preserve mutation without risking the organism.

## Practice 12 — Build a Barbell in Your Career

List your stable core.

Income.

Credentials.

Expertise.

Relationships.

Then list your experimental edge.

A new field.

A side project.

A language.

A publication.

A small business.

A technical skill.

A public body of work.

The core creates survivability.

The edge searches for new futures.

Review whether the edge is generating real evidence.

If it remains fantasy for years, either exercise it or close it.

Unused options consume attention too.

---

## Practice 13 — Calculate Your Runway

For a person or company, calculate how long you can operate if income drops sharply.

Do not use the optimistic budget.

Use the survival budget.

Then ask:

- Which obligations are fixed?
- Which costs can fall quickly?
- Which assets are liquid?
- Which income sources are independent?
- Which dependencies become urgent first?

Runway transforms vague financial anxiety into a decision horizon.

A longer horizon may justify a harder experiment.

A short horizon suggests reducing fixed commitments before adding speculative ones.

This is not financial advice about a specific portfolio.

It is strategic accounting for time.

---

## Practice 14 — Audit Identity Lock-In

Complete these sentences:

“I am the kind of person who…”

“My company is the kind of company that…”

“People like us never…”

“We always…”

Then examine each statement.

Is it a value?

A useful capability?

A habit?

A status defense?

A tactic masquerading as identity?

Identity helps coordinate behavior, but unnecessary identity claims narrow adaptation.

Try replacing brittle nouns with durable verbs.

Instead of “I am an executive,” try “I build and lead useful systems.”

Instead of “We are a hardware company,” try “We solve this customer problem with the best system we can build.”

Verbs travel better through changing worlds.

## Practice 15 — Preserve Dissent as a Conditional Model

When someone disagrees with the dominant forecast, do not merely record “Alex disagreed.”

Ask the dissenter to specify:

- what assumptions differ,
- what evidence would support the minority view,
- what indicators should be monitored,
- what action should change if those indicators appear.

Now the dissent becomes operational.

The organization does not need to debate forever.

It can commit to the majority plan while preserving a trigger for reopening the decision.

This is how disagreement becomes optionality instead of faction.

---

## Practice 16 — Price Lock-In Explicitly

When choosing vendors, technologies, contracts, locations, or financial structures, add a line item for switching cost.

Estimate:

- migration time,
- retraining,
- data conversion,
- legal penalties,
- downtime,
- lost relationships,
- specialized equipment,
- integration complexity,
- political or reputational cost.

The cheapest current option may be expensive after switching cost is recognized.

Conversely, paying a premium for flexibility can be wasteful when the technology is stable and migration easy.

Make the lock-in visible before it becomes invisible infrastructure.

---

## Practice 17 — Ask Who Owns the Upside and Who Bears the Downside

Every optionality system has distribution.

A company may keep flexibility by transferring insecurity to contractors.

An investor may cap losses while society absorbs environmental damage.

A government may experiment on citizens who cannot opt out.

A platform may preserve strategic freedom by locking developers in.

Before praising optionality, ask:

**Whose optionality?**

Then ask:

**Whose fragility?**

Good system design tries to align decision rights, upside, and downside without making experimentation impossible.

This is an ethical constraint on strategic cleverness.

---

## Practice 18 — Convert Surprises Into Assets

After an unexpected event, record not only what went wrong but what the surprise taught.

Customer misuse.

Unexpected demand.

A weird failure mode.

A competitor move.

An employee workaround.

A regulatory interpretation.

A technical bottleneck.

Ask:

What assumption did reality reject?

What new option became visible?

What should we test next?

Surprise is expensive if it becomes only embarrassment.

Surprise becomes an asset when it changes the future action set.

---

## Practice 19 — Close Zombie Options

Some options are not alive.

They are obligations wearing the costume of possibility.

The course you will never finish.

The product nobody owns.

The partnership nobody uses.

The abandoned domain name.

The strategy repeatedly postponed.

The relationship maintained only by guilt.

The “someday” project that consumes psychic bandwidth.

Review stale options.

Either:

- exercise,
- renew deliberately,
- transfer,
- archive,
- or close.

Optionality has carrying cost in attention even when financial cost is tiny.

A cleaner option portfolio improves commitment.

---

## Practice 20 — Use the Preserve-Learn-Select Loop

For any uncertain system, write six verbs on the page:

**Preserve.** Keep several credible futures alive.

**Learn.** Generate information cheaply.

**Select.** Kill what evidence weakens.

**Commit.** Concentrate resources where the thesis strengthens.

**Compound.** Stay long enough for capability, trust, and scale to build.

**Reopen.** When the environment changes, restore exploration before the old commitment becomes a cage.

This loop is the operational heart of the book.

It prevents two opposite failures.

Permanent exploration.

Permanent lock-in.

The healthy system breathes between them.

# Ten Paradoxes of Optionality

## Paradox One — Constraints Create Freedom

A budget limits spending and makes experimentation survivable.

A constitution limits government and preserves civic action.

A technical interface limits implementation and enables substitution.

A promise limits behavior and creates trust.

The right constraints remove destructive options so more constructive options remain possible.

## Paradox Two — Redundancy Can Be Efficient Over Time

A backup appears wasteful in ordinary conditions.

Its value is revealed across a distribution of conditions.

Efficiency measured at one moment can become inefficiency across decades.

## Paradox Three — Commitment Can Increase Optionality

Learning a difficult skill closes alternatives during training but opens more afterward.

Building trust limits opportunism but expands collaboration.

Good commitments generate future choices faster than they destroy present ones.

## Paradox Four — Independence Requires Interdependence

No person or nation becomes resilient by doing everything alone.

A diverse network of reliable relationships can create more autonomy than isolation.

The issue is not dependence itself.

It is dependence without substitution, reciprocity, or voice.

## Paradox Five — Failure Can Preserve Capital

A cheap failure that stops an expensive mistake is a successful information event.

Organizations that punish every failure encourage large hidden failures later.

## Paradox Six — Exit Can Deepen Loyalty

The ability to leave can make staying meaningful.

Coerced loyalty is stable only until coercion fails.

Chosen loyalty can become identity and trust.

## Paradox Seven — Diversity Needs Selection

Variation creates possibilities.

Selection creates adaptation.

Celebrating variety without evaluation produces noise.

Evaluating without variety produces stagnation.

## Paradox Eight — Liquidity Is Sometimes an Offensive Asset

Cash looks defensive until a crisis reprices opportunities.

Buffers do not merely protect against bad futures.

They create the ability to act in unusual futures.

## Paradox Nine — Planning Matters Most When Plans Fail

The value of planning is not the document.

It is the shared models, trigger points, dependencies, and alternatives uncovered during the process.

A good planning process creates adaptation capacity even when the forecast is wrong.

## Paradox Ten — The Best Option Is Often the Ability to Learn Faster

Specific predictions expire.

Learning capability compounds.

When uncertainty is irreducible, speed of adaptation can be more valuable than confidence in the initial plan.

# Final Exercise — The Room With Doors

Draw a rectangle representing your current situation.

Career, company, institution, family, project, or strategy.

Draw every credible door you can see.

Next to each door write:

- carrying cost,
- upside,
- downside,
- reversibility,
- expiration date,
- information likely to arrive,
- capability required to exercise it.

Then mark three categories.

**Maintain.** Worth keeping alive.

**Exercise.** Evidence is strong enough to commit.

**Close.** No longer worth the carrying cost.

Finally ask one more question:

**What door could we build that does not exist yet?**

That question is the creative frontier.

Optionality is not only choosing among available futures.

It is inventing new futures worth choosing.
